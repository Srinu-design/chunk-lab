1. Why chunk at all

4 min

Say you are building a help-desk bot for an online store. A customer asks: "How long do I have to return a damaged item?"

The answer is somewhere in the store's help manual, and the manual is thousands of words long. So why not paste the whole manual into the prompt? Every model has a limit on how much it can read at once. Even when that limit is large, a long prompt costs more, takes longer, and hides the one sentence you need among hundreds you don't. The fix is to cut the manual into small pieces, called chunks, and send only the few that matter. Below, the reading window is made tiny on purpose so you can see what happens when the text doesn't fit:

What the model can read at once (small window for demo) ⚠️ Overflow
Shipping Policy
Orders ship within two business days from our warehouse in Coimbatore... Delivery takes three to five days.

Returns Policy (General)
Unused items can be returned within 30 days of delivery in their original packaging...
Dropped · Model cannot read past here

...Damaged items can be returned within 60 days if you upload a photo of the damage...

Help-Desk Bot Response ❌ Wrong answer
User Question
How long do I have to return a damaged item?
Bot Answer
You can return items within 30 days of delivery in original packaging.

Why it failed: Because the document exceeded the demo reading window, the specific rule for damaged items was dropped. The bot guessed from the general 30-day policy instead.

1. Context Limits

Every model can read only so much at once. Anything past the limit is simply lost.

2. Retrieval Precision

One answer hidden in a long prompt is easy to miss. A few chunks that match the question keep the model on the right sentence.

3. Cost & Speed

You pay for every word you send, and long prompts are slower. Small chunks keep each question cheap and quick.

3. Fixed-size chunks

8 min

The simplest way to chunk is to count words: every N words, start a new chunk. It is fast, but it doesn't know where a sentence ends. If the answer sentence sits on a boundary, half of it lands in one chunk and half in the next, and neither chunk can answer the question. Overlap helps: each chunk repeats the last few words of the one before it.

Move the size and overlap sliders, or edit the Python directly. The two stay in sync. Each chunk is drawn as a block, and the answer sentence is underlined, so you can see whether it stays in one piece.

4. Smarter splits

8 min

Counting words cuts wherever the count runs out, often in the middle of a sentence. A recursive splitter follows the shape of the text instead. It first splits on blank lines (paragraphs). Any piece that is still too long is split on line breaks, then on sentence ends, and only as a last resort on single words.

Starting Settings by Document Type

No single chunk size fits every document. These are sensible places to start. Test them on your own documents before you trust them:

Document Type Recommended Split Why this works
Customer Support FAQs By section or 100–300 words Each Q&A pair is self-contained. Splitting mid-answer destroys the solution.
Legal Contracts By clause with generous overlap Clauses frequently reference definitions elsewhere; overlap carries context.
Source Code By function or class (never mid-function) Cutting inside a function breaks syntax trees and program execution context.

The Core Metric

Always check: does the top chunk contain the whole answer? If the answer is cut in half, even a strong model has to guess, and a guess can sound right while being wrong.

A note on tokens: Production models count tokens rather than words. For English text, a dependable rule of thumb is that 1 token ≈ 0.75 words (or 100 words is approximately 133 tokens). Always convert your word targets into token limits when configuring vector databases.
Prove: If your top retrieved chunk contains 80% of an answer sentence, is retrieval successful?
